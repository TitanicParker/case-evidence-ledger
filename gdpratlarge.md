# The Whole Story: Clinical Uncertainty, the Medical Record, and the GDPR Trail

There are really two documentary histories in this case. For years they appear to be separate. One is medical: how an unusual young-onset Parkinsonian presentation, severe foot problems, gait disturbance and genuine structural pathology were understood and managed from 2017 onward. The other is regulatory: how, beginning in 2025, the patient used the GDPR to obtain information, challenge the accuracy and completeness of the medical record, restrict disputed processing, and test the institutional account against the underlying documents.

Read separately, each history raises difficult questions.

Read together, they describe something more coherent.

The clinical record begins with an unresolved relationship. A practical management structure then becomes established around that uncertainty. Over the following years, new neurological, pharmacological and ultimately genetic information repeatedly bears upon the original unresolved question. Treatment changes locally, and individual symptoms acquire new interpretations, but the surviving documentary record does not show with equal clarity that the original case was reconstructed as a whole.

Then, in 2025, the patient begins using data-protection law to address essentially the informational consequence of that history. Article 16 is used to challenge the accuracy or completeness of the representation. Article 15 is used to obtain the underlying material with which that representation can be tested. Article 18 is invoked when the disputed information continues to have operational significance. Those exercises of rights themselves generate a second chronology involving routing, deadlines, refusals, alleged misclassification of rights, continued processing and eventually regulatory complaints.

The important point is not that the GDPR proves medical negligence. It does not.

Nor does the medical history prove a GDPR infringement.

The importance lies in the intersection: **what the institution's record said, what the institution knew, when it was put expressly on notice that the representation was disputed, what information the patient was able to obtain, and what the institution continued to do thereafter.**

That is the whole story.

## 1. What entered Neurology in 2017

The repository begins deliberately before the hospital's eventual diagnostic categories had taken control of the narrative.

The contemporaneous GP history did not present two neatly separated problems called “Parkinsonism” and “feet.” It recorded bilateral foot problems and abnormal gait, followed by increasing difficulty initiating movement, muscle tension, increased tone in both legs, worsening dexterity, functional decline and eventual direct neurological assessment.

That does not mean every foot abnormality had one neurological cause. The repository repeatedly guards against that inference.

It means that **the presentation entered Neurology in an integrated and unresolved state**.

During the July 2017 admission, two important propositions could coexist. The patient had severe young-onset Parkinsonism. The patient also had genuine structural foot pathology.

The hospital was therefore not confronted with a choice between “neurological” and “structural.” Both were real.

The crucial documentary fact is what happened to the relationship between them.

The hospital recorded unusual lower-limb and gait findings, including a footdrop-like appearance suggestive of dystonic rather than straightforward weakness, alongside the structural foot abnormalities. Yet the relationship between the neurological and foot domains was not finally adjudicated. It was recorded as **“Not clear.”** The repository treats that uncertainty as the true starting point of the case.

Importantly, the repository does not call that uncertainty negligent.

A clinician is permitted not to know something. Medicine regularly proceeds under uncertainty. Patients can have several pathologies simultaneously, and different specialties can reasonably treat different components while the precise causal relationships remain unsettled.

The problem arises from what happened afterward.

## 2. Uncertainty at the level of explanation became certainty at the level of operation

Although the relationship remained unresolved, practical care had to proceed.

Neurology retained responsibility for the Parkinsonian disorder. Podiatry became the practical pathway for the feet. Dopamine replacement was deliberately deferred because of the patient's unusually young age and concerns about long-term treatment complications. Parkinson's disease became an operative clinical and administrative category.

Again, none of those decisions is automatically improper.

The repository's central proposition is subtler: **operational allocation became definite before the underlying relationship became definite.**

That distinction matters enormously.

The Podiatry referral cannot itself subsequently be used as proof that the feet had been demonstrated to be neurologically unrelated. The contemporaneous GP record complicates that proposition because it recorded Podiatry as prioritising the case **“as Parkinson's.”** The repository therefore adopts the symmetrical rule that the Podiatry pathway proves neither neurological causation nor neurological separateness.

That is a very important discipline in the case.

The claimant does not need to say:

> Podiatry should never have been involved.

The defence cannot safely say:

> Podiatry was involved, therefore the foot problem had already been established to be independent of the neurological disorder.

Both propositions go too far.

The defensible documentary position is that **parallel pathways arose while the relationship remained unresolved**.

## 3. A durable treatment strategy followed

At roughly the same time, a fairly durable neurological treatment strategy became established.

Dopaminergic treatment was deliberately held back. The record contained a contemporaneous rationale: the patient was extremely young for Parkinsonism and there were concerns about exposing him to long-term treatment complications.

That strategy may have been orthodox and reasonable. The case does not need to prove otherwise.

But the same record also acknowledged the counterweight. Dopamine replacement would probably eventually become necessary. It could produce substantial benefit. There was even recognition of the possibility of later regretting that it had not been introduced earlier.

The documentary asymmetry identified in the repository is therefore not simply “treatment was delayed.”

It is this:

**the record explains why the established strategy should continue more clearly than it explains what would require the strategy to be reopened.**

There is no single explicit documentary “escape condition” saying, in effect:

> If X happens to the lower-limb phenotype, or if Y occurs during medication manipulation, or if function deteriorates beyond Z, the original assumptions must be reconstructed.

That does not prove no pragmatic clinical threshold existed. The adverse-case audit expressly preserves that defence. Clinicians need not write one formal rule governing every future reconsideration.

But it is part of the documentary architecture.

A similar pattern appears with genetics.

PARK2/PRKN was not a theory invented with hindsight after genetic confirmation. It was a contemporaneous branch of the case. Professor Healy independently considered PARK2 likely, and Galway later continued to identify PRKN as a suspected cause. At the same time, the value of genetic clarification was sometimes discussed partly in terms of whether the result would alter current management.

That may also have been clinically reasonable.

But it raises an important informational question: **what happens when the value of new information is measured by whether it changes a management plan that is itself based on an incomplete or unresolved explanatory model?**

That question becomes increasingly important as later evidence accumulates.

## 4. The later clinical evidence did not stay neutral

The case then changes over time.

Procyclidine materially altered the recorded neurological state. The patient improved while receiving it and deteriorated during withdrawal. Critically, the withdrawal episode generated information involving gait, tension and a lower-limb/foot-specific phenomenon.

That does not prove that a corn, structural deformity or every pain episode was neurological.

The repository expressly preserves several alternative mechanisms: altered tone, changed loading, movement, pain perception, structural pathology or combinations of them.

What matters is that **neurological medication state had begun to influence the same anatomical and functional domain whose relationship to the Parkinsonian disorder had originally remained unclear**.

In 2020, the documentary record becomes still more pointed.

A treating neurologist observed a foot movement resembling foot drop. Relevant ankle power was normal. The phenomenon was expressly characterised as actually Parkinsonian.

Again, that does not retrospectively establish that this 2020 manifestation was identical to everything seen in 2017.

But it is plainly capable of bearing on the old question.

A footdrop-like lower-limb phenomenon resembling one of the earlier features had now been directly interpreted by Neurology as Parkinsonian.

Then levodopa finally entered the case.

By 2021, aches, pains and stiffness were part of the clinical context in which Sinemet was introduced. Later that year, **“re-emerging dystonia and altered gait”** were expressly used to justify increased dopaminergic treatment.

By 2023, the relationship had become more explicit still. Significant leg or foot pain, shuffling and reduced mobility were documented in relation to medication timing, and the clinician assessed the leg pain and shuffling as OFF-related. A medication alteration was then made as a response-generating trial: change morning dopaminergic coverage and see whether the clinical state improved.

By 2025, severe morning pain itself had entered the dopaminergic treatment logic. Sinemet CR was being used partly against that morning state, and later documentation acknowledged substantial or profound pain relief.

None of this proves an exclusively neurological pain generator.

Indeed, a mixed-mechanism account may ultimately be the most persuasive: structural disease, abnormal loading, dystonia, OFF-state phenomena, neuropathic components and central dopaminergic modulation could all have coexisted.

The repository does not fear that complexity.

It uses it.

A mixed-mechanism case makes the original binary operational partition less, rather than more, capable of answering the whole problem.

## 5. Local updating versus global reconstruction

This becomes the central medical-documentary issue.

The later record clearly responds to information.

This is not a story in which clinicians did nothing.

Medications were changed. Symptoms were reconsidered. A foot phenomenon was labelled Parkinsonian. Dystonia and altered gait informed prescribing. Leg pain and shuffling became OFF-related. Morning pain became a target for dopaminergic coverage.

Those are real clinical updates.

But they appear predominantly as **local updates**.

A dose changes.

A particular symptom acquires a different interpretation.

A medication trial is introduced.

One lower-limb event is reclassified.

What is much harder to find is an equally explicit later reconstruction asking:

What did all of this mean for the original 2017 relationship?

Which historical manifestations remained structural?

Which had acquired a neurological explanation?

Which were mixed?

Which remained uncertain?

Did the original Neurology/Podiatry division still adequately describe the case?

Did later dystonia and OFF-state reasoning alter the historical foot narrative?

What did the increasingly specific PRKN explanation do to the original conceptual model?

That is P024, probably the most important proposition in the repository: **the surviving record shows local updating more clearly than global reconstruction of the original case.**

The defence audit is important here because it prevents that proposition being overstated.

There may have been informal reconsideration that was never written down.

The clinicians may have believed the local adjustments sufficiently incorporated the new information.

The absence of a reconstruction document does not prove the absence of thought.

Those possibilities remain.

The documentary proposition is narrower: the surviving record does not represent global reconstruction with anything like the clarity with which it represents local treatment changes.

Whether an ordinarily competent consultant neurologist was required to do more, and when, remains an expert question.

## 6. Genetics later confirms that one early branch was real

The eventual PRKN confirmation adds another layer but must be handled carefully.

It does not prove that every earlier foot symptom was neurological.

It does not prove that genetic testing should necessarily have been completed earlier.

It does not prove that starting levodopa earlier was mandatory.

Its significance is historical and epistemic.

The PRKN/PARK2 branch existed contemporaneously. It was independently strengthened by another specialist. It remained a specific suspected cause. It was eventually confirmed.

Therefore, the early genetic branch was not an irrelevant side hypothesis later rescued by hindsight.

That matters particularly where later accounts represented the historical diagnosis simply as “idiopathic Parkinson's disease.” The proposition register does not accuse anyone of dishonesty. It accepts that “idiopathic” could have been used loosely or at syndrome level. But an idiopathic-only historical representation does not reproduce the entire etiological history in which PRKN had been a live and ultimately correct branch.

And that brings the story to 2025.

Because by then the problem was no longer confined to what clinicians had once thought.

The **record itself** became contested.

## 7. 12 June 2025: the clinical informational problem becomes a formal data problem

On 12 June 2025, the patient submitted a formal Article 16 GDPR request to the HSE.

The request identified the medical diagnosis, the historic severe bilateral foot pain, the contention that the primary presenting symptom had not been adequately incorporated into the diagnostic account, later dopaminergic responsiveness, and the claim that the resulting record was inaccurate, misleading or materially incomplete. It requested rectification or annotation of relevant records and preservation of associated material.

Some of the language in that early request was stronger than the later evidential architecture would now support. For example, medication response by itself cannot safely “disprove” every peripheral or structural mechanism.

But the significance of the request does not depend on every proposition in it ultimately being right.

Its significance is that, on a fixed date, **the controller was expressly notified that the historical medical representation was being challenged**.

That creates a new kind of evidential boundary.

Before the Article 16 request, a negligence lawyer trying to establish institutional knowledge must substantially reconstruct what clinicians knew or should have known from the evolving clinical evidence.

After the Article 16 request, another proposition exists:

> The institution was directly told that the accuracy or completeness of the historical representation was disputed, and it was told the grounds on which that dispute was being advanced.

That does not make those grounds correct.

It proves notice.

And it is here that the medical and GDPR histories first truly converge.

The clinical case asks whether new evidence should have caused the original model to be reconstructed.

The Article 16 process says, in effect:

**the data subject is now formally asking the institution to reconstruct the representation itself.**

## 8. Article 15 turns the accuracy dispute into an evidence problem

Article 16 alone cannot answer what actually happened historically.

That requires source material.

The patient therefore began using Article 15 to obtain underlying data.

The important July 2025 request concerned the bedside teaching interaction around the 2017 admission and sought student, ward-round, educational, supervisory and associated documentation.

The hospital's 5 August response said that various requested categories were not held and also made an important organisational disclosure: Podiatry at Merlin Park belonged to Community Healthcare West rather than the hospital service, and Podiatry records had to be sought through that separate route.

That response later became part of the DPC material because the access question was no longer abstract. The hospital had taken substantive positions about what information existed, what it did not hold and which organisational entity held other relevant records.

This reveals the different functions of Articles 15 and 16.

**Article 16:** the representation is alleged to be inaccurate or incomplete.

**Article 15:** show the underlying personal data with which that allegation can be tested.

That distinction is particularly important here because the unresolved medical question concerns the relationship between different parts of a longitudinal presentation.

A later lawyer cannot safely resolve that by assertion.

The referral documents matter.

The Podiatry records matter.

The communications between specialties matter.

The wording used at different dates matters.

The chronology of diagnosis and record finalisation matters.

The GDPR access process therefore becomes an evidential mechanism.

## 9. Article 18 introduces the question of post-notice use

A third right then becomes important.

The patient invoked Article 18 restriction while the accuracy dispute remained unresolved.

That changes the focus from the historical content of the record to its **continuing operational use**.

By February 2026, the patient was putting material before the DPC showing that a Neurology appointment had been generated during a renewed Article 16/18 engagement. The submission expressly asked the Commission to treat the event not as a new clinical complaint but as evidence of continuing operational processing within the disputed neurological pathway during the live accuracy/restriction period.

Whether that particular processing was legally permissible is for the appropriate regulatory analysis.

But evidentially, the question has changed again.

It is no longer merely:

> Was the historical diagnosis or narrative right?

It is:

> **What was the institution doing with the disputed information after the accuracy dispute had been formally placed before it?**

This is where the “before and after” idea becomes genuinely useful.

The GDPR does not retrospectively transform an earlier clinical judgment into negligence.

But formal rectification, annotation, supplementation or even documented notice of a live accuracy dispute can create identifiable dates after which institutional conduct may need to be assessed against a changed information state.

That can be extremely important in later litigation.

## 10. The DPC complaint itself matures

The DPC complaint, C-25-7-2074, also evolves.

It begins against a background of contested accuracy and Article 16 handling. Over time the material supplied to the Commission becomes more structured.

There are complaints about access.

There are disputes about statutory timing.

There are Article 18 issues.

There are allegations that rights were being misclassified or treated as though a different administrative request had been made.

There is evidence of continued processing.

There are renewed Article 16/18 exercises rather than reliance solely on what happened in June 2025.

By 2026, the patient was sending primary correspondence to the Commission and deliberately separating the HSE/GUH complaint from the University of Galway, which the DPC had clarified was a separate controller matter. The HSE/GUH file remained C-25-7-2074; the University matter proceeded separately.

That separation is important because it prevents the pattern argument from becoming indiscriminate.

Different institutions may hold copies or derivations of the same medical history but remain different controllers with different responsibilities.

The broader significance is not that every difficulty can be attributed to one person or one organisation.

It is that **a disputed medical representation can propagate across institutions**, creating a succession of access, accuracy, rectification and restriction problems.

That becomes visible in later engagements with the Ombudsman, Medical Council, PCRS and University.

But the central HSE/GUH complaint remains analytically distinct.

## 11. The 2026 Podiatry SAR returns to the original 2017 fork

The Podiatry SAR is where the circle nearly closes.

The repository says that one of the decisive events in 2017 was the operational allocation:

**Neurology — Parkinsonian syndrome.**

**Podiatry — foot pathology.**

The relationship remained unresolved.

The 2025 GUH access response then told the patient that the Podiatry records were not part of the hospital service and directed him to Community Healthcare West.

On 15 July 2026, the patient followed that route.

The Article 15 request was specific. It sought the Podiatry/Chiropody records relating to the Merlin Park pathway from 2017 onward: referral information, referral processing, clinical notes, assessment and treatment, appointments and waiting-list material, discharge or closure information, communications with Neurology, the GP or other HSE services, and associated photographs, diagrams or testing.

This was not a request asking the HSE to discover what unknown service might have been involved.

The 2025 process had already identified the service.

On 20 July, the HSE acknowledged receipt of the Podiatry SAR and requested identification. The patient supplied government-issued identification eight minutes later.

On 23 July, HSE Consumer Affairs assigned the reference **SAR 2026-983** and expressly referred to the Subject Access Request **“issued to this office on July 15th 2026.”** It confirmed that the request had been forwarded to the General Manager of Primary Care Services Galway **“for processing.”**

That is a very clean administrative trail.

It also has an unusually precise relationship to the medical case.

The clinical argument asks what became of the foot side of the unresolved 2017 presentation after it was operationally allocated to Podiatry.

Nine years later, the patient is using Article 15 to obtain the documentary record of **that exact pathway**.

The importance of the Podiatry records cannot be predetermined.

They might show that Podiatry treated an entirely coherent structural problem and achieved sustained improvement.

They might show that abnormal movement persisted despite structural treatment.

They might contain communications back to Neurology.

They might show that clinicians regarded some symptoms as neurological.

They might show nothing particularly useful.

They might materially assist the defence.

That uncertainty must be preserved.

But the access request is nonetheless logically central to the evidence architecture.

It seeks the information capable of testing one of the repository's foundational propositions.

The one-month period from the HSE's acknowledged 15 July date expired on 15 August 2026. The correspondence trail supplied so far contains no substantive Article 15 response and no extension notice. The patient has chosen to wait until close of business on 17 August before taking the next formal step.

If nothing arrives, the next event can be handled in three clean layers: notification to DPO West of the expired period; a simple DPC complaint concerning SAR 2026-983 itself; and, separately, a request that the Commission consider whether the new event belongs within a broader pattern of rights-handling difficulties.

That last question should not be forced.

The individual SAR failure is strongest when kept simple.

The pattern case should be allowed to emerge from the accumulation of separately proved events.

## 12. The case now contains three different forms of knowledge

This may ultimately be one of the most useful features for lawyers.

The combined chronology creates at least three different knowledge dates.

The first is **clinical constructive knowledge**.

At what point should an ordinarily careful consultant neurologist, looking only at the evidence then available, have reconsidered the original foot–neurology relationship?

That requires expert evidence.

Perhaps the answer is not 2017.

It might arise after Procyclidine supplied lower-limb medication-state information.

It might become stronger after the 2020 Parkinsonian-foot observation.

It may become difficult to avoid once dystonia, altered gait, OFF-related leg pain and medication-responsive morning pain enter the clinical reasoning.

An expert must answer that.

The second is **documented institutional notice**.

Whatever clinicians should previously have appreciated, the institution can demonstrably be shown to have received an explicit accuracy challenge by June 2025.

That is not constructive knowledge.

It is documentary notice.

The third is **post-notice processing knowledge**.

Once access, rectification and restriction rights had been invoked, what did the controller do?

What did it search?

What did it disclose?

What did it refuse?

What did it annotate?

How did it classify the request?

What continued to be processed?

What deadlines were met?

Which were missed?

Those are administrative and regulatory facts capable of proof largely without neurological expert evidence.

The three layers should never be collapsed.

Their usefulness comes from being different.

## 13. What the case still does not prove

Despite the remarkable convergence, several boundaries remain essential.

The documents do not prove that every foot symptom was Parkinsonian.

They do not prove that structural foot pathology was insignificant.

They do not prove that Podiatry referral was negligent.

They do not prove that levodopa had to be started in July 2017.

They do not prove that the Procyclidine response or later Sinemet response uniquely identifies the cause of earlier pain.

They do not prove that PRKN confirmation retrospectively explains every symptom.

The absence of an explicit global reconstruction document does not prove that no clinician ever reconsidered the overall case.

The Article 16 request does not itself prove that the disputed diagnosis or record was inaccurate.

The DPC does not become a medical-negligence tribunal simply because clinical material appears in a GDPR complaint.

The Podiatry SAR delay does not prove that records are missing, destroyed, concealed or damaging to the HSE.

And repeated procedural problems affecting the same patient do not by themselves prove deliberate targeting or organisation-wide systemic failure.

Those limitations are not weaknesses to be hidden.

They are what make the surviving argument credible.

## 14. Why the combined case is unusual

The potentially important feature is not simply that a medical-negligence case and a GDPR dispute happen to involve the same patient.

It is that **the subject matter of the GDPR dispute is the informational architecture created by the medical history itself**.

The medical record began with uncertainty.

The institution developed a durable management structure around it.

Later information repeatedly qualified the original relationship.

The surviving record appears to update individual components more clearly than it reconstructs the whole.

The patient then invokes Article 16 against the accuracy and completeness of that representation.

He invokes Article 15 to obtain the material with which the historical representation can be tested.

He invokes Article 18 because the disputed information remains operational.

He brings the resulting handling to the regulator.

And eventually he seeks the records of the Podiatry pathway created at the original 2017 fork.

That is why the two stories cannot now be understood entirely apart.

The clinical record asks:

> **What did the organism actually do, and what should competent clinicians have made of the accumulating evidence?**

The data-protection record asks:

> **What did the institutional information system say that history meant, and what happened when the data subject formally challenged that representation?**

The repository itself captures the distinction elegantly:

**“What the organism did versus what the record said the organism meant.”**

That may ultimately be the governing sentence for the combined case.

A negligence action concerns the first question.

An accuracy and access dispute concerns the second.

But in longitudinal healthcare the two can interact because the medical record is not merely an archive of past thought. It is information used to organise present care.

Once later evidence changes the informational landscape, continued reliance on the old representation can become a separate question from whether the original judgment was defensible when first made.

And once the data subject formally invokes statutory rights, the institution acquires documented opportunities to examine, qualify, disclose and control that information.

That is the “before and after” effect.

## 15. Where the story stands

The medical case is now capable of being stated narrowly.

The patient entered neurological care with severe Parkinsonism, genuine structural foot disease and an unresolved relationship between the neurological and lower-limb presentations.

Parallel care was not inherently wrong.

The stronger question arose later.

Neurological medication states began producing lower-limb information. A footdrop-like phenomenon was subsequently labelled Parkinsonian. Dystonia and altered gait entered dopaminergic prescribing decisions. Leg pain and shuffling were interpreted as OFF-related. Morning pain became a target of dopamine replacement. The contemporaneous PRKN branch was ultimately genetically confirmed.

The record documents those individual developments.

What remains much less explicit is when the original **“Not clear”** relationship was reconstructed globally.

Whether competent neurological practice required that reconstruction, when it should have occurred, what it would probably have changed and whether earlier reassessment would probably have reduced a measurable period of severe pain and functional impairment are matters for independent expert evidence.

The GDPR story begins where the record problem becomes explicit.

From June 2025 onward, the institution is no longer dealing solely with whatever clinicians might independently infer from the medical chart. It is dealing with a patient formally asserting that the representation is inaccurate or incomplete, asking to see the underlying data and subsequently seeking restriction of disputed processing.

The institutional responses to those rights create a second, independently provable chronology.

And the Podiatry SAR brings that chronology directly back to one of the unresolved medical questions with which the story began.

That does not make the ultimate outcome inevitable.

It makes the case unusually well structured.

There is a clinical evidence problem.

There is a record-accuracy problem.

There is an access problem.

There is a restriction and continuing-processing problem.

There is a regulatory-handling problem.

And there are identifiable dates at which those problems begin to overlap.

The potentially significant question is therefore not whether the GDPR can be used to “prove a misdiagnosis.”

It is this:

> **What obligations arise when a healthcare institution continues to rely on a historical medical representation, subsequent clinical evidence materially qualifies the assumptions underlying that representation, and the data subject then formally invokes rights of access, rectification and restriction to force the discrepancy into explicit institutional consideration?**

That question has significance beyond this patient.

But the case does not need to be called a landmark case now.

The better approach is the one already embedded in the repository:

**source first, fact second, proposition third, argument last.**

If the clinical expert evidence ultimately supports the reassessment theory, if the GDPR chronology remains as clean as the primary correspondence presently suggests, and if the missing Podiatry material can be obtained and assessed without preconception, then the combined record will give a regulator, lawyer or court something unusual: not merely a retrospective allegation, but a longitudinal documentary account of how an unresolved clinical relationship became an institutional information structure, how later evidence challenged it, and what occurred when the patient finally used statutory information rights to demand that the structure itself be examined.

That is the whole story as it presently stands.
