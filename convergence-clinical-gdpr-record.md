# The Convergence of the Clinical Record and the GDPR Record

The significance of this case becomes much clearer when two documentary histories are placed beside one another.

The first is the **clinical history** reconstructed in the case-evidence repository. It concerns an unresolved relationship between Parkinsonism, gait abnormality and severe bilateral foot/lower-limb problems, the practical division of that presentation between Neurology and Podiatry, and the accumulation over subsequent years of neurological, pharmacological and genetic evidence capable of bearing on that original uncertainty.

The second is the **data-protection history** beginning in June 2025. It concerns the patient's attempts, through Articles 15, 16 and 18 GDPR, to obtain the underlying information, challenge the accuracy or completeness of the medical representation, and restrict continuing use of disputed information while its accuracy remained unresolved.

These are not the same legal case. Medical negligence cannot be proved by GDPR non-compliance, and GDPR rights do not empower the Data Protection Commission to decide retrospectively whether a clinician's diagnosis was negligent. But the two histories intersect at a point of considerable importance: **the institutional representation of an unresolved medical history, the knowledge available to the institution, and what happened when that representation was formally challenged.**

## The clinical starting point: uncertainty followed by operational certainty

The repository deliberately avoids an exaggerated medical theory.

It does not claim that every historical foot symptom was neurological, that the patient did not have genuine structural foot pathology, that referral to Podiatry was inappropriate, or that levodopa necessarily should have been introduced in 2017.

Its governing documentary proposition is narrower. The contemporaneous record begins with one evolving presentation involving the feet, gait, difficulty initiating movement, muscle tension, increased bilateral leg tone, worsening dexterity and functional deterioration. During the July 2017 admission, severe young-onset Parkinsonism and genuine structural foot pathology were both documented. Importantly, the relationship between the neurological and lower-limb/foot domains was expressly recorded as **“Not clear.”**

That uncertainty did not prevent care from proceeding, nor should it necessarily have done so.

Neurology continued to manage Parkinsonism. Podiatry became the practical pathway for the feet. Dopaminergic therapy was deliberately deferred because of the patient's young age and concern about long-term treatment complications. Parkinson's disease became an operative administrative and clinical category.

The repository's argument is therefore not that this initial allocation was negligent. It is that **the medical relationship remained unresolved even though the management architecture became definite**.

That distinction becomes increasingly important because the clinical record did not remain static.

Procyclidine materially affected the neurological state, and withdrawal generated lower-limb and foot-specific information. In 2020, a foot movement resembling foot drop was observed with preserved relevant power and expressly described as Parkinsonian. Levodopa entered treatment in 2021, and later dystonia and altered gait were used to justify increased exposure. By 2023, severe leg pain, shuffling and reduced mobility were being assessed in relation to medication timing, with leg pain and shuffling expressly regarded as OFF-related. By 2025, dopaminergic treatment was being used partly against the severe morning pain state. The long-standing PARK2/PRKN hypothesis was ultimately genetically confirmed.

None of those events proves a unitary mechanism for every foot symptom. The repository is emphatic on that point.

Their significance is cumulative. They repeatedly supplied information capable of bearing on the very relationship that had originally been left unresolved.

This produces the repository's central distinction:

**the later record shows local updating much more clearly than it shows global reconstruction.**

Medications changed. Individual symptoms were reinterpreted. A foot phenomenon was labelled Parkinsonian. Dystonia and gait influenced prescribing. Leg pain and shuffling entered OFF-state reasoning. Morning pain became a target of dopaminergic treatment.

What is much more difficult to identify is a later reconstruction asking what all of those developments meant for the original 2017 relationship.

That is proposition P024: the represented record documents local therapeutic and interpretive updating more clearly than an explicit reconstruction of the original foot–neurology relationship.

The independent adverse-case exercise does not destroy this proposition. It substantially limits what can be inferred from it: parallel treatment may have been entirely reasonable; medication response does not establish a unique mechanism; PRKN confirmation does not retrospectively neurologise every structural foot problem; and absence of an explicit reconstruction document does not prove that clinicians never reconsidered the case. Nevertheless, the underlying documentary sequence survives those qualifications.

That is where the clinical record stood before GDPR entered the picture.

## 12 June 2025: the informational problem becomes explicit

The Article 16 request of 12 June 2025 creates an important dividing point.

The patient formally told the controller that his medical data were disputed. He identified the severe foot history, challenged the adequacy of the diagnostic representation, referred to later treatment responsiveness, sought correction or annotation of relevant records and requested preservation of associated material. 

The merits of that particular rectification request remain legally distinct from the medical-negligence question. A historical diagnosis does not automatically become inaccurate merely because later evidence changes the clinical understanding. Nor does Article 16 empower a patient to require deletion of the historical fact that a clinician held a particular opinion.

But something important happens irrespective of the ultimate outcome of the rectification request.

**The institution has now been expressly placed on notice of the disputed informational relationship.**

Before 12 June 2025, a later lawyer considering institutional knowledge might principally have to ask what clinicians should have inferred from the evolving clinical record.

After 12 June, there is documentary evidence of something more concrete: the controller was directly told what aspect of the historical representation the patient considered inaccurate or incomplete.

That does not prove that the patient was correct.

It proves that the dispute existed and that the controller knew it existed.

This creates the first important convergence between the two histories.

The medical case asks:

**When should accumulating clinical evidence have caused the original unresolved relationship to be reconsidered?**

Article 16 asks:

**Once the data subject says that the resulting representation is inaccurate or incomplete, what does the controller do with that challenge?**

The questions are different, but they concern the same informational architecture.

## Article 15: moving from assertion to evidence

Article 15 adds a second dimension.

The patient's later access requests were not merely repetitions of the Article 16 demand. They sought the underlying material with which disputed history could be tested.

The 7 July 2025 Article 15 request concerning the 2017 bedside teaching interaction ultimately produced the GUH response of 5 August. That response itself became relevant to the existing DPC complaint because of what the hospital said it did and did not hold, the boundaries between controllers and services, and the degree to which the underlying historical event could be reconstructed. 

Conceptually, Articles 15 and 16 therefore perform different functions within this history.

Article 16 says:

**the representation is disputed.**

Article 15 says:

**show the data from which the representation and the underlying history can be tested.**

This is particularly important in a medical case because a dispute about an old clinical narrative cannot safely be resolved from memory alone. Referral material, contemporaneous notes, communications between services, discharge documentation, treatment records and later annotations can determine whether a later account accurately reproduces what was actually known at an earlier time.

GDPR therefore becomes not merely a mechanism for requesting correction, but a mechanism for recovering the evidential substrate on which the accuracy question depends.

## Article 18: the dispute becomes operational

Article 18 changes the problem again.

Once accuracy remained contested, the patient invoked restriction. By February 2026, material was being placed before the DPC documenting continuing operational activity in the Neurology pathway during a renewed Article 16/18 period. The patient's submission expressly presented a newly generated Neurology appointment as evidence of ongoing operational processing while the accuracy and restriction position remained unresolved. 

Again, it is essential not to prejudge the legal result. The existence of an Article 18 request does not necessarily mean every subsequent use of data was prohibited.

But the evidential question changes.

The issue is no longer simply:

**What did the historical record say?**

It becomes:

**What was the institution continuing to do with the disputed record after the dispute had become formally known?**

That distinction could become important far beyond data-protection proceedings.

A lawyer examining subsequent clinical or administrative conduct may be able to distinguish between reliance on information before formal notice and reliance after the institution had been expressly told that the accuracy or completeness of the relevant representation was contested.

GDPR has therefore created something that ordinary retrospective medical litigation often struggles to establish cleanly: **dated institutional knowledge events.**

## The Podiatry SAR closes an extraordinary circle

The July 2026 Podiatry request is especially significant when placed beside the repository.

One of the repository's six carrying propositions is P004: operational allocation of the Parkinsonian syndrome to Neurology and the foot problem to Podiatry occurred while the relationship between those domains remained unresolved.

P005 then adds an important qualification. The existence of the Podiatry pathway cannot itself prove neurological separateness, particularly because the contemporaneous GP record indicated that Podiatry was prioritising the case **“as Parkinson's.”**

The Podiatry records therefore potentially matter to a very specific historical question:

**What actually happened within the foot pathway after that operational allocation was made?**

The patient first learned through the 2025 access process that GUH regarded Podiatry at Merlin Park as belonging to Community Healthcare West and directed him to obtain those records through the community route.

On 15 July 2026 he followed that route.

The request was narrow. It sought referral and referral-processing information, Podiatry assessments and treatment, appointment and waiting-list information, closure or discharge material, communications with Neurology, the GP or other HSE services, and associated foot records.

In other words, it sought precisely the type of documentation capable of reconstructing the Podiatry half of the original operational allocation.

HSE acknowledged the request, requested identification, received it immediately, assigned reference SAR 2026-983 and confirmed on 23 July that the request issued to the office on 15 July had been forwarded to the General Manager of Primary Care Services Galway for processing.

The statutory period expired on 15 August.

If no substantive response or extension is received by the end of 17 August, the Article 15 event becomes established on its own terms.

That does not establish what the Podiatry records contain.

It does not establish concealment.

It does not establish that the records would support the patient's clinical case.

The documents could strengthen the claimant case, substantially qualify it, or assist the defence.

But the alignment is nevertheless remarkable.

In 2017, an unresolved presentation was operationally divided between Neurology and Podiatry.

In 2026, after years of later neurological evidence and after a formal Article 16 accuracy dispute, the patient uses Article 15 to recover the documentary history of the Podiatry side of that division.

The statutory access period then expires without that material being supplied.

The clinical and GDPR histories have effectively arrived at the same point from opposite directions.

## Three distinct knowledge boundaries

The combined chronology therefore creates at least three potentially important temporal boundaries.

The first is **clinical constructive knowledge**.

At what stage did the cumulative medication-state, gait, dystonia, OFF-state and other neurological evidence become sufficient that an ordinarily competent consultant neurologist should have reconsidered the original unresolved relationship?

That question requires independent neurological expert evidence.

The second is **documented institutional notice**.

By what date can it be proved that the institution was expressly told that the historical representation was challenged as inaccurate or incomplete?

The June 2025 Article 16 correspondence provides a firm documentary event.

The third is **post-notice data handling**.

Once Articles 15, 16 and 18 had been invoked, how did the institution respond? What information did it disclose? What did it correct or annotate? What did it refuse? What processing continued? What statutory deadlines were met or missed?

These three boundaries should not be collapsed into one another.

Their power comes from their independence.

An expert can determine what should have been appreciated clinically.

The correspondence can establish what was actually put to the institution and when.

The GDPR history can establish how the controller dealt with the challenged information thereafter.

## Why the combined case may matter beyond one patient

This is where the matter has a potentially broader significance.

The usual description of Article 16 as a right simply to “correct a record” understates what can happen when it is applied to continuing healthcare.

A medical record is not merely historical evidence. It can be an operating informational model. It influences referrals, appointments, prescribing, eligibility, communication between clinicians and the way later professionals understand earlier events.

Where later evidence materially qualifies that informational model, the question of accuracy is therefore not purely archival.

And where the data subject formally challenges the model, seeks the underlying data and invokes restriction while accuracy is being considered, GDPR begins to create a regulatory structure around the same informational problem that may already exist clinically.

The potential wider question is consequently not:

**Can GDPR be used to overturn a doctor's diagnosis?**

It is much more sophisticated:

> **Where a healthcare controller continues to use a historical medical representation for present purposes, what obligations arise when subsequent information materially qualifies that representation and the data subject formally invokes rights of access, rectification and restriction in order to expose and resolve the discrepancy?**

This case is unusual because the underlying clinical record already contains the seeds of that question.

The repository does not begin from a simplistic allegation of obvious misdiagnosis. It begins from documented uncertainty: **“Not clear.”**

It then follows a durable operational architecture and later information capable of bearing upon that uncertainty.

Years later, the GDPR process independently forces the institution to confront the accuracy, completeness, availability and continuing use of the resulting information.

That is the convergence.

The medical case asks whether the clinical model should have been reconstructed as evidence accumulated.

The GDPR case asks what happened when the patient eventually demanded that the **information model itself** be examined.

Neither proves the other.

But together they may provide something unusually powerful: a documentary history of an unresolved clinical problem, followed by a documentary history of the patient's attempts to use statutory information rights to force that problem into explicit institutional consideration.
