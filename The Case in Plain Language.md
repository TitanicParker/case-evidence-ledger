# The Case in Plain Language

This case is not built on the claim that every problem with the patient’s feet was caused by Parkinsonism. It does not depend on saying that Podiatry should never have been involved, that levodopa had to be started in 2017, or that later genetic findings somehow prove what every earlier symptom meant.

The argument is narrower.

The patient entered neurological care with a presentation that had not yet been divided into two settled and unrelated conditions. He had serious bilateral foot problems, but he also had an unusual gait, increasing stiffness, difficulty initiating movement, increased muscle tone, brisk reflexes, worsening dexterity and significant functional decline. The reason Neurology became involved was not simply that Parkinsonism had already been identified. It was because the overall presentation required explanation.

During the 2017 admission, two things became clear at the same time.

First, the patient had severe young-onset Parkinsonism.

Second, he also had genuine structural foot pathology.

Those facts were capable of coexisting. Structural disease did not disappear simply because Parkinsonism had been diagnosed, and the Parkinsonian syndrome did not make every foot symptom neurological.

But the important point is that the relationship between those two domains was not actually resolved.

The hospital record itself described a striking gait with the appearance of foot drop but suggested that it looked dystonic rather than like true weakness. At the same time, there were real structural abnormalities in the feet. The relationship between the neurological findings and the foot pathology remained uncertain.

That uncertainty is the starting point.

It is not necessarily the breach.

## The practical division of care

The clinicians still had to do something.

Neurology continued to manage the Parkinsonian syndrome. Podiatry became the practical pathway for the feet. Dopaminergic treatment was deliberately deferred. Parkinson’s disease became an operative administrative and clinical category.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that.

Clinical care often has to proceed before every causal relationship has been settled. A patient can have two genuine conditions at once. Different specialties can reasonably manage different parts of the same presentation.

The argument therefore does not say that the original division of care was improper.

It says something more careful:

**the medical uncertainty remained unresolved even though the practical management became divided.**

That distinction becomes important later.

The Podiatry pathway cannot itself prove that the foot symptoms had been shown to be neurologically separate. In fact, the contemporaneous GP record referred to Podiatry prioritising the case “as Parkinson’s”, which makes the later meaning of that referral more complicated, not less.

The position in 2017 can therefore be stated fairly.

The patient had real structural foot disease. He had real Parkinsonism. The relationship between the two was uncertain. Parallel care was reasonable. The unanswered question was what later evidence would do to that original uncertainty.

## A treatment strategy became established

The neurological management plan also became fairly durable.

Dopamine replacement was deliberately held back because the patient was unusually young and there were concerns about long-term motor complications. That may have been entirely orthodox clinical practice.

The argument does not depend on proving otherwise.

But the same record also recognised that dopamine replacement would probably eventually be needed, might provide substantial benefit, and could later raise the question of whether it should have been introduced earlier.

That creates an important issue.

The record contains a clear reason for maintaining the existing strategy. What is much less clear is the threshold at which that strategy would have to be fundamentally reconsidered.

In plain terms:

**what would have to happen before the clinicians went back and reopened the overall explanation?**

A similar issue arises with genetics.

The possibility of PARK2/PRKN disease was not invented years later. It was a live and specific possibility relatively early in the history. Professor Healy independently thought PARK2 was likely, and the Parkin/PRKN branch remained part of the clinical reasoning.

At the same time, the value of obtaining a firm genetic answer was sometimes discussed partly in terms of whether that answer would alter immediate management.

Again, that may have been clinically defensible.

But it raises a wider question about the relationship between information and an already stable management plan.

If new information is judged mainly by whether it changes the existing plan, there is a risk that information capable of challenging the assumptions behind that plan is given too little weight.

That is not yet a conclusion of negligence. It is a question about how the case was being reasoned through.

## Then the evidence began to change

The argument becomes stronger because the case did not remain frozen in 2017.

New information accumulated over time.

The first important example was Procyclidine.

The patient improved significantly on the medication. When it was withdrawn, his neurological condition worsened. Importantly, the deterioration included a lower-limb and foot-related phenomenon alongside increasing tension and worsening movement.

That observation does not prove that the foot pain was caused by Parkinsonism.

There are several possible explanations. Neurological medication might alter tone, loading, posture, movement, pain perception, or some combination of those things.

But the important fact is that the neurological treatment state had begun to affect the very lower-limb domain that had previously been left unresolved.

That should matter to the original question.

## The 2020 foot phenomenon

In 2020, the treating neurologist observed a lower-limb “foot throw” that resembled foot drop.

The relevant muscle power was preserved.

The phenomenon was then described as actually Parkinsonian.

That observation still does not prove that the 2020 movement was identical to every symptom present in 2017.

It does something more limited.

It shows that a lower-limb phenomenon resembling one of the earlier features was later directly interpreted by Neurology as part of the Parkinsonian disorder.

That is another piece of evidence bearing on the old uncertainty.

## Levodopa and the evolving lower-limb picture

By 2021, levodopa entered the treatment regimen.

As treatment evolved, the records increasingly connected medication state with stiffness, gait, dystonia, lower-limb symptoms and pain.

By late 2021, re-emerging dystonia and altered gait were being used expressly as reasons to increase levodopa exposure.

Again, this does not prove that every foot complaint was neurological.

But it means that the clinical picture was becoming harder to describe as two stable and independent domains.

The neurological treatment was increasingly relevant to the lower-limb state.

## By 2023, the connection became more explicit

By 2023, there were records of significant leg pain, shuffling and reduced mobility in relation to medication timing.

Most importantly, the leg pain and shuffling were expressly interpreted as OFF-related.

A medication change was then used in a way that was capable of generating information about whether the symptoms improved with altered dopaminergic coverage.

At that point, the issue was no longer simply whether Parkinsonism and foot pain happened to coexist.

The clinical team itself was treating at least some of the lower-limb symptoms as related to medication state.

That matters because the original relationship had never been conclusively resolved in the first place.

## By 2025, the morning pain itself had entered the Parkinson’s treatment logic

By 2025, Sinemet CR was being used partly with the severe morning pain state in mind.

Later documentation accepted that Sinemet had been helping the pain for some time and described profound relief after treatment escalation.

There is an important alternative explanation, and it should be preserved.

Parkinson’s medication may reduce the experience of pain even where the original pain generator is structural or peripheral.

That is entirely compatible with a mixed-mechanism model.

The patient could have had structural foot pathology, altered loading, dystonic movement, OFF-related symptoms, neuropathic pain and central modulation of pain at the same time.

The case does not need to force those possibilities into a single explanation.

In fact, the possibility of several mechanisms makes one question more important:

**was the original relationship ever properly reconsidered in light of what was being learned?**

## The central problem

The later records show real clinical responsiveness.

Treatment changed.

Symptoms were reinterpreted.

A foot phenomenon was described as Parkinsonian.

Dystonia and gait changes affected medication decisions.

Leg pain and shuffling were regarded as OFF-related.

Morning pain became something dopaminergic treatment was deliberately being used to address.

These are meaningful updates.

The problem is that they appear mostly as updates to individual pieces of the case.

A medication was increased.

A symptom was reclassified.

A treatment trial was introduced.

A particular lower-limb phenomenon was given a neurological explanation.

What is much harder to identify is a later point at which the original overall question was explicitly reconstructed.

In other words:

**what did all of this later evidence mean for the unresolved relationship recorded in 2017?**

Did the original division between Neurology and Podiatry still describe the case adequately?

Which symptoms still appeared structural?

Which had acquired a neurological explanation?

Which appeared medication-responsive?

Which remained uncertain?

Did the later dystonia and OFF-state evidence require the historical account to be revised?

Did the evolving PRKN evidence affect the explanatory model?

The central documentary concern is therefore not that nothing changed.

A great deal changed.

The concern is that **individual parts of the case were repeatedly updated without the record showing, with the same clarity, that the original overall relationship was rebuilt.**

## Why the later PRKN result matters

The eventual genetic confirmation of PRKN disease adds another layer.

It does not prove that every historical foot symptom was dystonic.

It does not convert corns, calluses, deformity or structural disease into neurological findings retrospectively.

Its significance is different.

The genetic branch existed contemporaneously. PARK2/PRKN had been considered years earlier and independently supported. It was eventually confirmed.

That means the earlier genetic reasoning was not a meaningless side path.

The ultimate result gave causal specificity to the movement disorder and confirmed that an important part of the early explanatory model had been pointing in the right direction.

That makes the absence of a clear later reconstruction of the historical case more noticeable, but it does not remove the need for careful symptom-by-symptom analysis.

## What the argument does not claim

Several limits are essential.

The argument does not establish that every historical foot symptom was neurological.

It does not establish that Podiatry was an inappropriate referral.

It does not establish that levodopa should have been started in July 2017.

It does not establish that medication response uniquely identifies the cause of pain.

It does not establish that the later PRKN result explains every earlier symptom.

It does not establish that any clinician acted dishonestly.

And it does not establish, from the documents alone, that the care was negligent.

Those questions require further evidence.

## The negligence question

The documentary argument and the negligence question must be kept separate.

The documents can show that an unresolved relationship existed and that later evidence repeatedly bore upon it.

The next question belongs to an appropriately qualified neurological expert:

**At what point, if any, would an ordinarily careful consultant neurologist have been expected to reconsider that unresolved relationship in light of the information then available?**

That is the important question.

The case should not begin by assuming that the answer is 2017.

The original uncertainty may have been entirely reasonable.

The stronger question may arise later, once the evidence had accumulated.

If an independent expert concludes that competent neurological practice required a structured reassessment by some identifiable point, the next question is what that reassessment would probably have led to.

Would the patient have undergone more systematic medication-state assessment?

Would the lower-limb phenotype have been reformulated?

Would treatment have been adjusted earlier?

Would the medication-responsive component of the pain have been identified earlier?

Only then does the argument reach causation.

## The harm has to be incremental

The injury is not Parkinsonism itself.

It is not PRKN disease.

It is not the existence of structural foot pathology.

Those conditions were not caused by any alleged failure of care.

The possible compensable harm is narrower:

**the additional period of severe pain, impaired mobility, disrupted activity and related suffering that would probably have been avoided or materially reduced if the required reassessment had occurred earlier.**

That period cannot simply be assumed to run from 2017.

It must be supported by expert evidence.

A shorter, well-supported period of avoidable suffering would be a stronger case than an exaggerated claim that every year of pain was preventable.

## The argument in one paragraph

The patient entered neurological care with a combined and unresolved presentation involving severe foot problems, abnormal gait and emerging Parkinsonism. The hospital identified both genuine structural foot disease and severe neurological disorder but left the relationship between them unclear. Practical care was nevertheless divided, with Neurology managing Parkinsonism and Podiatry managing the feet. Over the following years, increasingly relevant evidence emerged: neurological medication altered the lower-limb state, a later foot phenomenon was described as Parkinsonian, dystonia and gait changes influenced levodopa treatment, leg pain and shuffling were interpreted as OFF-related, morning pain became a target of dopaminergic therapy, and the long-standing PRKN hypothesis was eventually genetically confirmed. None of those facts proves that every foot symptom was neurological. The central question is whether, as that evidence accumulated, the original unresolved relationship was ever adequately reconsidered as a whole. The records show repeated local responses to new information much more clearly than they show a global reconstruction of the original case. Whether that amounted to negligent care, and whether earlier reassessment would probably have reduced the patient’s suffering, are questions that require independent expert evidence.

That is the argument.
